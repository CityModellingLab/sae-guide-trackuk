A practical guide to Small Area Estimation for Urban Analytics

A practical tutorial for Bayesian small area estimation using R-INLA

Urban research often needs estimates at fine geographic scales: neighbourhoods, small administrative areas, transport zones, or other local geographies. Survey data are rarely collected at that resolution. Once responses are split by area and time, many cells have small samples, high uncertainty, or no observed respondents at all.

This tutorial introduces spatio-temporal small area estimation (SAE) as a practical workflow for this problem. The aim is to show how survey microdata, direct estimates, auxiliary area data, population information, and spatial-temporal model structure can be combined to produce small area estimates with uncertainty.

1 Why Bayesian inference with INLA?

The tutorial uses Bayesian small area estimation models because they provide a coherent way to combine direct survey evidence, auxiliary predictors, spatial structure, temporal structure, and uncertainty. This is especially useful when some MSOA-year cells have small samples or no observed respondents.

The model-based examples are fitted with INLA, the R interface to Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation. INLA is well suited to this workflow because it provides fast Bayesian inference for latent Gaussian models, including hierarchical, spatial, temporal, and spatio-temporal random effects without relying on long MCMC workflows.

2 Tutorial Workflow

The worked example uses the National Survey for Wales to estimate:

Share of adults who walk for transport daily by MSOA-year in Wales over three waves: 2021, 2023, and 2025.

Note

The MSOA-level survey geography used here is synthetically prepared for tutorial purposes. The resulting maps and findings should not be treated as true small area estimates for Wales. More information is provided in the data preparation step.

  1. Prepare data
    Build the survey indicator, matched weights, MSOA-year domain, area predictors, unit predictors, and poststratification inputs.

  2. Direct estimation
    Produce survey design-based small area estimates and sampling variances. This establishes the baseline and shows where direct estimates are too sparse or noisy.

  3. Fay–Herriot area-level model
    Model the direct estimates and their sampling variances using area predictors, temporal effects, and spatial smoothing.

  4. Battese–Harter–Fuller unit-level model
    Model survey microdata directly, then aggregate the fitted relationship to MSOA-year estimates using known area-level predictor means.

  5. Multilevel regression and poststratification
    Fit a richer unit-level model and average predictions over population poststratification cells, so the final estimates better reflect the target population structure.
